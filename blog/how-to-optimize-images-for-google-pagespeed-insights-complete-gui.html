Why Google PageSpeed Insights Cares About Images

Google PageSpeed Insights measures how well your page performs for real users. Images are often the heaviest assets on any webpage, and when they're not optimized, they trigger multiple warnings including:

  • Serve images in next-gen formats – recommending WebP or AVIF over JPEG/PNG
  • Efficiently encode images – flagging images that are larger than necessary
  • Properly size images – identifying images served larger than their display size
  • Defer offscreen images – suggesting lazy loading for below-the-fold images

Each of these warnings eats into your overall score. Fixing them can push your performance score from the red zone into the green, which directly impacts your Core Web Vitals, user experience, and even your Google search rankings.

Step 1: Choose the Right Image Format

The format you use for your images has a massive impact on file size and quality. Here's a quick breakdown of the most common formats and when to use them:

JPEG

Best for photographs and complex images with lots of colors. JPEG files compress well without sacrificing too much visual quality. Always compress JPEGs before uploading to your website.

PNG

Best for images that require transparency or sharp edges, like logos and icons. PNG files tend to be larger than JPEGs, so compression is especially important here.

WebP

This is Google's preferred format. WebP images are typically 25–35% smaller than equivalent JPEGs and PNGs while maintaining excellent quality. PageSpeed Insights will specifically recommend converting your images to WebP whenever possible.

AVIF

The newest next-gen format, AVIF can compress images even more efficiently than WebP. Browser support is growing rapidly, making it a smart future-proof choice.

Step 2: Compress Your Images Before Uploading

Compression is the single most effective way to reduce image file sizes. There are two types of compression to understand:

  • Lossy compression – reduces file size by permanently removing some image data. The quality loss is usually invisible to the human eye at moderate compression levels.
  • Lossless compression – reduces file size without losing any image data. The file will always be somewhat larger than lossy alternatives but retains full quality.

For most website images, lossy compression at 75–85% quality delivers the best balance between file size and visual quality. You can compress image online free using tools like MakeImageSmall, which makes it incredibly simple to reduce image sizes without installing any software.

Step 3: Resize Images to Their Display Dimensions

One of the most common mistakes website owners make is uploading massive images and then shrinking them with HTML or CSS. For example, uploading a 4000px wide photo and displaying it at 800px forces the browser to download all of that unnecessary data.

Always resize your images to the actual dimensions they'll be displayed on screen before uploading. If your blog content area is 800px wide, your images should be no wider than 800px. Combined with compression, this can reduce file sizes by 80% or more.

Step 4: Use Lazy Loading for Below-the-Fold Images

Lazy loading tells the browser to only load images when they're about to enter the user's viewport. This significantly improves initial page load time because the browser doesn't waste resources loading images the user may never see.

Adding lazy loading to your images is as simple as adding a single HTML attribute:

  • Use loading="lazy" on your img tags for any images that appear below the fold
  • Never apply lazy loading to your hero image or above-the-fold images, as this can hurt your Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) score

Quick Image Optimization Checklist
