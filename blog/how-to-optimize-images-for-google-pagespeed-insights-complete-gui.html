Why Image Optimization Matters for Google PageSpeed Insights

Google PageSpeed Insights evaluates your website across a range of performance metrics, many of which are directly tied to how you handle images. When images are too large, poorly formatted, or improperly served, they trigger warnings like:

  • Properly size images – Images are larger than necessary for the screen size
  • Serve images in next-gen formats – Images aren't using WebP or AVIF
  • Efficiently encode images – Images haven't been compressed enough
  • Defer offscreen images ��� Images below the fold load before they're needed

Each of these issues adds unnecessary kilobytes or even megabytes to your page weight. According to Google, images account for the majority of downloaded bytes on most web pages. Fixing them can dramatically improve your Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), First Contentful Paint (FCP), and overall performance score.

2. Use the Right Image Format

Choosing the correct file format can cut your image size in half without any visible difference to the user:

  • WebP – The best modern format for web use; supported by all major browsers and typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG
  • JPEG – Best for photographs and images with many colors
  • PNG – Best for images with transparency or sharp edges like logos and icons
  • Avoid BMP, TIFF, and other heavy formats for anything displayed online

Google explicitly recommends serving images in next-gen formats like WebP. If your current tool doesn't support format conversion, look for a way to compress image online free that also handles format conversion in the same workflow.

3. Resize Images to the Correct Display Dimensions

Uploading a 4000×3000 pixel image when it's only displayed at 800×600 pixels wastes bandwidth. Google PageSpeed Insights will flag this under "Properly size images." Always resize your images to match the maximum dimensions they'll actually appear on screen before uploading.

A good rule of thumb: if your blog content area is 800px wide, your images should be no wider than 1600px to account for retina screens. Anything beyond that is unnecessary weight.

4. Enable Lazy Loading

Lazy loading tells the browser to only load images when they are about to enter the user's viewport. This improves initial page load time significantly, especially for content-heavy pages.

Adding lazy loading is as simple as including the loading="lazy" attribute in your image tags. Most modern CMS platforms like WordPress also offer built-in or plugin-based lazy loading.

5. Use a Content Delivery Network (CDN)

A CDN serves your images from servers geographically closer to your users, reducing latency and load times. Many CDNs also offer automatic image optimization, including compression and format conversion on the fly.

6. Add Width and Height Attributes

Specifying the width and height of your images in HTML prevents layout shift, which affects your Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) score. It allows the browser to reserve the correct amount of space before the image loads, improving visual stability.
